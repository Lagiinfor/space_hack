Guide to the SPHEREx Sky Flip-book

This page explains everything on the website: what you are looking at, what each button does, and what to try on the five NASA test targets. No astronomy or software background is needed.

How it works behind the scenes

1. The idea in one minute

SPHEREx is a NASA space telescope launched in 2025. It photographs the whole sky about every six months, in infrared light (heat light, invisible to our eyes), split into 102 colours instead of the 3 a normal camera uses.

Most things in the sky stay put. Things that are close to us (nearby stars, brown dwarfs, asteroids) shift position between photos taken months apart. Some stars also change brightness. If you flip between photos of the same patch taken at different times, anything that jumps or flickers stands out. That is how Pluto was discovered.

This website does that flipping for any patch of sky you choose, and adds things no older tool does: the spectrum (brightness at every infrared colour) of any object you click, in each sky pass, and molecule lenses that make objects with unusual chemistry (like methane) stand out.

2. Words you will see

WordMeaning
RA, DecRight Ascension and Declination: longitude and latitude on the sky, in degrees. RA runs 0–360°, Dec runs −90° (south pole) to +90° (north pole).
Arcsecond (″), arcminute (′)Angles on the sky. 1° = 60′ = 3600″. The full Moon is about 30′ wide. One SPHEREx pixel covers 6.15″.
ExposureOne single photo taken by the telescope.
Sky passOne full sweep of the sky, about 6 months. A spot is photographed many times during a pass, over a few days to weeks. So far there are 3–4 passes.
Detector / bandSPHEREx has 6 detectors, each covering a range of infrared wavelengths (band 1: 0.75–1.09 µm … band 6: 4.42–5.00 µm). µm = micrometre, a millionth of a metre. Visible light is 0.4–0.7 µm.
StackingAveraging many exposures of the same spot. Noise averages away, so faint things become visible.
SpectrumHow bright an object is at each wavelength. Molecules absorb specific wavelengths, so a spectrum is a chemical fingerprint.
Proper motionHow fast a star drifts across the sky, in arcseconds per year (″/yr). Most stars: well under 0.1″/yr. Barnard's Star: 10.4″/yr, the fastest known.
Brown dwarfA "failed star": too small to burn hydrogen. Cool, faint, glows mostly in the infrared, and its air contains methane and water.
GaiaA European space telescope that measured the positions and motions of nearly 2 billion stars. We use it to predict where stars should be.
mJy (milli-jansky)A unit of brightness used by astronomers. Bigger = brighter. Only the comparisons matter here.
SaturatedThe star is so bright that the centre pixels "burn out", like an over-exposed photo. Their values are lost.

3. Opening a patch of sky

  • Guided tour (top right): click one of the five NASA test targets. They are already saved on this computer, so they open in a few seconds. Each comes with a short story of what to look for.
  • Sky position box: type a position and press Show (or Enter). Three ways work:
    • degrees: 269.4464 4.7686
    • hours/minutes/seconds and degrees: 17 57 47.1 +04 46 07
    • a name: RW Aur, Vega, M42. The tool looks the name up and moves the catalogue position (which is for the year 2000) forward to 2026 using the object's known motion. The line under the box says what it found.
  • Field size (2.4′, 3.6′, 6′, 9′): how much sky is shown. Bigger shows more, which helps for fast movers, but downloads more.
  • The first time you open a new spot, the tool downloads every SPHEREx photo of it from NASA's archive: usually 250–550 photos, 2 to 4 minutes, shown with a progress bar. After that, the spot is saved on this computer and opens instantly.

4. Reading the picture

  • North is up, east is left (as when looking up at the sky). So an object moving west moves to the right.
  • The blue crosshair marks the position you asked for.
  • Top left: the sky pass shown (for example "Mar 2026"), how many photos were stacked, and the wavelength range.
  • Top right: compass and field size.
  • Move the mouse over the picture: the line under it gives the exact RA/Dec, the offset from the centre, the brightness at that pixel, and the nearest Gaia star if any.
  • White blobs are stars and galaxies. Big ones are bright. SPHEREx pictures look soft because its pixels are large (6″); that is normal.

5. The flip-book and its controls

Why the bands matter (the "fair flip-book"). SPHEREx has no ordinary filters. Each detector sits behind a filter whose colour changes across it, so every single photo sees a given star at a slightly different wavelength. If you flipped raw photos, stars would seem to brighten and fade just because the colour changed. So for each detector and each sky pass we stack all the photos (15–25 of them). Every frame in a band then covers the same wavelengths, and only real changes blink.

  • Wavelength band buttons: pick one of the 6 detectors, or All bands (all six averaged: the deepest picture). Cold objects like brown dwarfs show best in the longest bands (3.83–4.41 and 4.42–5.00 µm).
  • Sky pass buttons: jump to a pass. The coloured dot next to each pass is the colour used for it in the spectrum plot.
  • Play / Pause (or the space bar) and Prev / Next (or the ← → keys).
  • Blink all passes or Blink first ↔ last: the longest time gap shows motion most clearly.
  • Speed: how fast the frames change.
  • Brightness: slide right to make faint things brighter (bright stars will burn out); slide left to see detail in bright stars.
  • Faint stars: slide right to lift the faint end without burning out the bright stars.
  • If a pass has only a handful of photos in a band, it is too noisy to compare fairly. The tool skips it in the blink and says so in the label.

6. Difference views

The dropdown next to the play buttons changes what each frame shows:

  • Normal: the stacked picture.
  • Difference (minus average): each pass minus the average of all passes. Things that did not change disappear. Red = brighter than usual here, blue = fainter. A star that moved shows up as a red/blue pair (red where it is now, blue where it used to be) that flips as you blink. A star that changed brightness turns all red or all blue. Press d to toggle.
  • Change since first pass: each pass minus the first one.
  • All passes added: everything stacked together, the deepest single picture.

Small changes below the noise level are shown as dark grey, so the colours you see are real changes, not noise.

7. Click for a spectrum

Click any object in the picture. The click snaps to the centre of the nearest star. The Spectrum panel on the right then shows:

  • Dots: one per photo. Each is the object's brightness (in a 12″ circle, with the sky around it removed) at the wavelength that photo saw it. Error bars show the uncertainty.
  • Lines: one per sky pass, joining the typical (median) value in narrow wavelength steps. If the lines lie on top of each other, the object did not change. If they separate, it did.
  • Grey shaded strips mark where molecules absorb: CH₄ (methane, 3.3 µm), ice (water ice, 3.0 µm), CO₂ (4.27 µm), CO (4.67 µm). A dip in one of those strips hints at that molecule.
  • For moving objects, the measurement follows the object: it finds it again in each pass. Coloured dots on the picture show where it was found in each pass, and the panel says how far and which way it moved.
  • Log scale button: useful when the brightness spans a big range.
  • You can zoom the plot by dragging a box on it; double-click to zoom back out. Hover over a point for exact values.

Very bright stars. If the centre of the star is burnt out (saturated) in many photos, the tool switches to measuring the star's outer glow (a ring 18–37″ from the centre, which is not saturated), the same way in every photo, and says so in an orange note. The numbers are then not the star's total brightness, but passes can still be compared fairly.

8. Automatic mover search

Every time you open a spot, the tool searches it by itself (results in the Automatic mover search panel, and arrows on the picture; the Movers button hides/shows the arrows):

  1. It finds every point of light in each pass and measures its position precisely.
  2. It links each one to the same object in the other passes.
  3. Ordinary stars wobble a little from pass to pass because of measuring noise; that wobble tells the tool how precise this field is. Anything that moved steadily by many times that wobble is flagged.
  4. Each flagged object is checked against Gaia: KNOWN = Gaia predicts this motion; CANDIDATE = nothing in Gaia explains it, so it is worth a human look.

The arrow starts at the object's first position and points the way it moved. It is drawn 4 times longer than the real motion so that a few arcseconds are visible. The number is the speed in ″/yr.

9. Gaia stars overlay

The Gaia stars button (or g) draws a circle where the Gaia catalogue predicts each star should be on the date of the pass you are looking at, including its proper motion and the small yearly wobble caused by Earth going around the Sun (parallax).

  • Green circles: ordinary stars (brighter than Gaia magnitude 16.5).
  • Yellow circles with a dashed path and a speed label: fast movers (over 0.2″/yr).
  • When you blink, a fast star should stay inside its moving circle. If something moves and has no circle, Gaia does not know it.
  • The "This position" panel lists how many Gaia stars are in view and the fast ones.

10. Molecule lenses

Open the Molecule lenses tab. Each lens builds a false-colour picture from three wavelength ranges shown as red, green and blue. The colours are balanced so that ordinary stars look white. Anything with a colour has an unusual spectrum. Faint, noisy pixels are kept grey so that colour only appears where there is real signal.

LensRed / Green / Blue (µm)What stands out
Infrared colour3.0–5.0 / 1.6–3.0 / 0.75–1.6Cool or dusty objects look orange-red; hot stars bluish-white.
Methane4.1–4.65 / 3.2–3.45 / 1.95–2.1Green sits on methane's absorption. Methane-rich brown dwarfs lose green and look red/magenta. Glowing dust (3.3 µm emission) looks green.
Water ice3.55–3.9 / 2.9–3.15 / 2.2–2.45Stars behind icy clouds lose green and look magenta.

The numbers in brackets in the label are how many photos went into each colour. The dropdown under the lens buttons chooses all passes added together (deepest) or a single pass.

11. Problem flags and the exposure list

  • NASA marks every pixel of every photo that may be untrustworthy. The flags dropdown tints those pixels yellow for the pass on screen. The kinds are: saturated (too bright), cosmic ray / transient (a particle hit the detector), ghost / stray light (reflections from bright stars), satellite streak, bad / hot / dead pixel, and persistence (an afterimage of an earlier bright star). The note on the picture says in what fraction of photos the pixel was flagged. Use it to check whether something odd might be an artefact.
  • Under This position, the table lists the sky passes with their dates and number of photos. Open Exposures in the frame on screen to see every photo in the current frame: date and time, detector, and the exact wavelength it saw at this spot.

12. Sharing and saving

  • Copy share link: the web address remembers the position, field size, band, view, lens and Gaia setting. Paste it into the address bar later to come back to exactly this view. (For now it only works on this computer, because the website runs here.)
  • Save picture: saves what is on screen as a PNG image (into your Downloads folder).
  • Save animation (GIF): saves the blink as an animated GIF with labels, ready for slides.

13. The five test targets: what to try

Barnard's Star: the fastest star in the sky

  • Band 1.63–2.41 µm, press Play: the big bright star hops upward (north) between passes.
  • Turn on Gaia stars: it stays inside its yellow circle, which moves 10.4″/yr.
  • Choose Difference: a red/blue pair that flips.
  • The mover search finds it: KNOWN, about 10.3″/yr, matching Gaia.
  • Its centre is saturated, so its spectrum uses the outer glow (orange note).

Epsilon Indi B: a pair of brown dwarfs

  • Band 4.42–5.00 µm, Difference: a clear red/blue pair moving south-east (down-left), about 4.7″/yr. Four sky passes are available here.
  • Click it: the spectrum dips at 3.3 µm (methane) and peaks near 4 µm, the brown-dwarf fingerprint. The four pass curves agree.
  • The mover search: KNOWN (Gaia predicts it).

WISE 0855: the coldest known brown dwarf

  • Molecule lenses → Methane: it is the one red dot at the centre; every other star is white.
  • Flip-book, band 4.42–5.00 µm: a faint dot at the centre. In Difference it is a blue/red pair moving west (right), about 8″/yr.
  • The mover search flags it as a CANDIDATE: no Gaia star predicts this motion. It is too faint for Gaia, so the tool found it with no hint. This is exactly how a new object would be discovered.
  • It is passing a faint background star right now, so its spectrum shows both: the star's light at short wavelengths and the brown dwarf's bump at 4–5 µm.
  • Note: the coordinates printed in the challenge text are from about 2014. By 2026 it has moved about 1.6′ west, so the tour uses its present position.

RW Aurigae: a young star with a dusty disk

  • Click the star. It is too bright for the centre pixels, so the outer glow is measured.
  • Between Sep 2025 and Sep 2026 its glow at 3–5 µm faded to about half (the wavelengths of warm dust in its disk), while short wavelengths changed much less.
  • The mover search finds nothing moving: correct, it is a variable star, not a fast mover.

R Draconis: a pulsating red giant

  • Click the star. The June 2025 curve is clearly brighter than November 2025 and June 2026 across most of the spectrum. It swells and shrinks every 8 months or so.
  • The wavy shape of the spectrum comes from water vapour in the giant's cool outer layers.
  • It is extremely bright (centre burnt out over ~25 pixels), so treat the exact numbers as rough.

14. Honest limits

  • Saturated stars (Barnard's Star, RW Aur, R Dra): pictures and motion are fine; spectra use the outer glow and are approximate. On Barnard's Star, which should not change, passes agree to about 5–10% at most wavelengths (up to ~25% at the shortest), because the telescope's star shape differs slightly by season. Changes much bigger than that are real.
  • Only 3–4 sky passes exist so far, so very slow movers (under ~1″/yr) are hard to see.
  • Asteroids and comets move far faster than stars (they are gone between passes). This tool targets slow movers like nearby stars and brown dwarfs.
  • Spectrum brightness is measured in a 12″ circle: right for comparing passes and wavelengths, not a calibrated total brightness.
  • Lenses combine all passes, so a fast mover is slightly smeared.
  • If NASA's archive is busy, a new position can fail. Just press Show again.

15. How it works behind the scenes

  1. Find: asks NASA's IRSA archive which SPHEREx photos cover the position (about 1 second).
  2. Download: fetches a small cut-out around the position from each photo. Only the picture layers are downloaded (about 130 KB each instead of 4 MB), so a whole target is about 45 MB.
  3. Clean: removes pixels NASA flagged as bad and the glow of sunlight scattered by dust in the solar system (zodiacal light).
  4. Group: sorts photos into sky passes (a new pass starts after a gap of more than 60 days).
  5. Align and stack: redraws every photo on a common north-up grid, then averages all photos of one detector in one pass, throwing out one-off blemishes such as cosmic rays.
  6. On demand: spectra, lenses, flags, Gaia predictions and the mover search are computed from the saved photos when you ask.

Data: NASA/IPAC IRSA SPHEREx archive (data release 1 and quick release), ESA Gaia DR3, CDS Sesame name service. New SPHEREx data releases are picked up by adding their name in one place in the code.
